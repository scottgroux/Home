Memento Memoriam

The beauty and joy of memories is that they are emotional and fickle. 10 minute read

My recent time away from employment has enabled me to return to regular literature reading. This return has opened up a period of self rediscovery, what kind of book do I like? As a child, I would tear through books from classic children's literature of Harry Potter, to more deeply fantasy inclined tomes of Eragon and Lord of the Rings. But now 15 years gone since my last proper completion of a book without academic necessity, what would I find myself partial towards?

I decided to ride the unpredictable wave of "what English paperback is available on the hostel bookshelf" instead of preparing myself with an e-reader before departure. I found dry history books, page turning spy novels, netflix series inspiring romance stories, and incredibly abstract tales told by a certain Italian author. How Italo Cavino's books found their way into my hands as many times as they did is beyond me. Regardless, I've rediscovered a love of reading I hope to hold onto more tightly than before.

My most recent completion was Kafka on the Shore by Murakami. A fantastical multi plot story that slowly coalesces upon a single timeline, I found myself processing an idea that has been with me for over a decade. To avoid spoilers, the lead character, Kafka, finds himself in an alternate reality/purgatory where he spends every day with and is tended upon by the woman of his dreams. I say "woman of his dreams" in the most literal of ways possible. In addition, none of the people in this space hold memories. If they do hold memories, they fade away quickly for "the library takes care of those". He is implored by the woman to leave immediately, for if he does not, the opportunity to do so will disappear. All the character asks is "I want you to remember me. If you remember me, then I don't care if everybody else forgets."

When I was 21 years old, my close friend passed away, Alex Fish. I was in Denmark for a semester of university and he was in Spain doing the same. He lost control of his Type 1 diabetes one night and slipped into a coma, slipping away from myself and everyone else who loved him. It hurts to this day. I hold a tattoo of two fish on my ankle, one swimming behind the other. This is my daily reminder that one day, I will follow him into the abyss.

The saddest part of Fish's death was not the act itself. I was completely removed from what happened. He passed away on the other side of the continent and I was unable to attend the service back in the United States. I tried to process my anguish and grief and it was incredibly difficult. I went for several runs in the woods that ended in tears. I attended a Kygo concert in Spain with another mutual friend as a memorial of sorts to him. We had originally bought tickets as a group with Fish. Alcohol took over after the show and most of the rest of the night was lost. While unideal at the moment, this experience has served as a reminder of how not to deal with the passing of someone near and dear.

The worst part of Fish's passing that still sticks with me today is the knowing that all I have of him is done and complete. The photos, videos, memories I have will not continue to grow, as I thought they would as lifelong friends. As I've continued to process his passing even 10 years on, I've come to realize all we ever have of anyone is memories. Momentos, messages, sentiments, monuments, all of these are physical or intangible reminders of the humans we surround ourselves with. If you remember me, then it doesn't matter if everybody else forgets.

Some math: 30% of your life you should be sleeping, 25% of your life you are probably working. During the remainder, you are eating/washing/maintaining yourself or are (hopefully) with family/friends. The numbers indicate that a large portion of the time, you are not with the people you love and care for.

Conversely, a majority of the time you exist, you are but a memory to others of a time they spent with you. This isn't stated to cheapen your life and actions, but instead heighten the importance of presence and enjoyment when you are with others. The 1-3 hours you see a friend will represent the sentiment in their mind of yourself for the next 1-3 weeks. Realistically, those numbers are a best case scenario. Haven't seen a friend in multiple months? The importance only grows.

~

We sit at an uncomfortable inflection point as a society, as an animal species, of designing our way out of our fundamental flaw. Memories are fickle and fade over time. Our brains only maintain so many neural pathways, pruning away the ones of no use to keep us as fresh and quick as we can in the topics that matter. Eventually, neuroplasticity decreases and brain function isn't as clean as it once was. Recall slows down, stories from youth become tall tales, we get old.

What happens when we let the library take care of memories? Attempts to create great halls of knowledge spanning all of human history are as old as civilization itself. The most impactful knowledge consortium on my life thus far is Wikipedia. I loved Wikipedia as a child. To no surprise, I was very popular at school with plenty of friends, just like any other Wikipedia loving youth. After completing my homework, I would chase down rabbit holes, jumping from article to article, feasting upon the crowd sourced and volunteer monitored record of everything. I attribute some part of my inquisitive nature and curiosity to access to the internet during these formative years.

The Internet is a very different tool now that it was 20 years ago. Tech firms with offices across the globe have generated near trillion dollar valuations due to monopolizing your time spent on the Internet. Advertisements based on your search exist plastered on every page, preying on your deepest insecurities. A far cry from Wikipedia and its non-profit operating foundation. The Internet was once heralded on its potential to revolutionize and improve humanity through communication, interconnectivity, and access to information. Now, it is increasingly a tool for the global oligarchs to distract and sow division amongst the general populace.

I don't remember the last book I read for fun as a teenager. I do remember what came after that book very clearly. Already a teenager with a flip phone and an iPod nano, I lobbied my parents for an iTouch. After a summer of mowed lawns, I received my first black mirror in 7th grade. Books required keeping a light on, which could alert prying adult eyes to the fact that you weren't asleep. Full color games on a backlit screen? It wasn't even a competition.

Since then, the screens have gotten bigger, the flip phone became redundant, and the attraction became more than just Flappy Bird. I became a big fan of social media from the get-go. The premise of sharing photos, thoughts, memes, and cat videos instantly clicked. Away messages on AOL, updating my page theme on MySpace, commenting on Reddit post discussions, sharing angsty music lyrics as statuses on Facebook. To do all of this while in the private seclusion of my bedroom, away from the same prying eyes surrounding the family desktop computer, was a surreal experience during childhood.

At the beginning, the tech companies weren't thinking about curation of content via algorithms. They were full of potential, buoyed by investment capital, unbound by expectations of positive cash flows. Around the end of my high school years, Facebook started having advertisements. At the end of college, Instagram was no longer a chronological feed. The beauty and simplicity of social media disappeared. Instead of seeing your friends and family posts from today, you see what They want you to see.

Social media feeds became my daily reading, or so I told myself. I supplemented Twitter threads with email newsletters, "long form" opinion pieces, and the news of the day to form a "healthy" written content diet. I didn't read books, because why focus on one topic when you could read small bits of 100 topics? My education forced me to read enough books, the idea of adding more to my plate seemed masochistic and the very last thing I wanted after studying.

The same notion held into the beginning of my professional life. You would think Peace Corps volunteer service would have been a nice time to recalculate and reassess, but news and basketball podcasts filled the empty spaces between teaching and playing soccer. Maybe returning home to no job, no daily schedule might open up an afternoon for a quiet story under a tree? It's difficult to make productive decisions when 80% of your day is staring into the void of despair about how to recreate the plan you once had for your 20s. Surely, once settled with a white collar 9-5 job, just a spy novel to liven things up? Reading and writing emails was all I did all day, any added tasks in those categories would have to have a better reason than just because.

This approach to long form writing and reading for personal enjoyment hurt me. What I previously believed was a prudent, balanced approach to content intake was heavy on processed and refined media that was designed to spark my engagement. It didn't take days, weeks for me to complete a well written piece and ponder on in-between. There's benefit to be gained from sitting on a bus, ruminating on what has happened in a fiction tale. Even better to guess what will happen next, when the story is not tied to the constraints of reality.

There's inherent difficulty in looking at a blank word document and filling it with thoughts. Your ideas and theses are improved when you don't have to press send within 20 minutes. These reading and writing tasks improve our critical thinking, test our written communication, and develop memories in our mind that we call upon later in life. I have spent so much time away from it that I will sadly never get back. At the same time, coming back now is better than never, and it feels like returning to a sport after years of inactivity. The movement isn't natural and I've lost a step from where I once was. But with each step, it gets a little easier.

Now, the Library of Everything has been greatly improved. Gone are the Wikipedia days of struggling to find the correct page for your question at hand, then interpreting the jargonese into plain speech. 24 hours a day, there's a desk worker there to help you find exactly what you need. There's a mathematician and statician, present to churn out any statistic necessary for your argument. There's an expert in every field of research ever, possessing the most patient of attitudes, willing to explain in as many ways necessary for you to understand the concept at hand. Every book, every photo, every note, every comment on the Internet has been incorporated into the greatest library mankind has ever seen. All thanks to the same people who ruined the Internet.

The question becomes why sit and struggle through a book? The library has every book and they are all summarized neatly, with someone waiting to explain it to you. Why struggle through writing an email? The library can help you write far more quickly and has amazing grammar. Why work on writing all your ideas out? Your ideas are probably very similar to someone else's from before and, especially considering the library already knows your writing tone from scraping all the content you've put on the Internet from your adult life, we could do a pretty bang up job on your 2000 word piece in 30 seconds or so. Worst case, it will be a wonderful rough draft that you can make edits to and claim all the credit. Isn't the library magical?

Artificial Intelligence and Large Language Models exist and pretending they have no positive value to society isn't productive for anyone. They have their use cases and will be another tool to increase productivity, similar to the computer. Caution should stem from their potential to negatively affect our ability to read, write, think, and remember. Never before has something existed that could make the need to recall ideas or become knowledgeable in a topic redundant. Critical thinking skills decay if neglected and huge divisions within society would manifest if vulnerable people are not protected and sheltered from A.I.'s impacts.

Additionally, there should be extreme mistrust of the people who have developed these tools and their reasons for doing so. The developers behind all of the major LLM's have the same incentives as the social media giants of the past 15 years. They are looking to make money and there will soon be very real and present impetuses for these technologies to make money and provide shareholder value. Social media has created divisions in society via echo chambers, allowed the spread of misinformation, and fomented violent ideas that have manifested into genocide. We gave the social media companies the opportunity to police themselves and they failed miserably. We cannot make the same mistake again.

The opportunity to responsibly adopt is here and now. Humanity should defend the sanctity of its collective memories with vigor. We have achieved one of the great technological feats that has escaped us for centuries and there is real power in said technology. The resulting power from this tool cannot be concentrated in the hands of few.

We cannot lose sight of the fact that remembering is what creates joy, astonishment, sadness, anger, and love. Remembering the dearest people in our lives and how they make us feel when we are with them is how they stay alive within us. To be human is to remember. If we instead choose to let the library take care of our memories, we might not remember the path to go back.
